---
title: Rubric for User Studies
---

A good user study should have these qualities:

- **Purpose.** The task list addresses important questions about the design. Each rationale explains what watching a participant could reveal that testing the app yourself would not.
- **Tasks and setup.** Tasks describe believable user goals without giving away the steps or interface labels. They cover the app’s important interactions at different levels of complexity. The data and starting state let participants attempt each task, and the report explains how the participants relate to the intended audience.
- **Conduct.** Participants consent to the study and understand their role. The facilitator encourages thinking aloud, uses neutral prompts, and allows participants to work through difficulties. Reports identify any help given, so readers can distinguish independent success from success with assistance.
- **Evidence.** Reports describe specific actions, comments, and outcomes. They show where participants struggled, recovered, or succeeded, and use debriefing to explore what participants expected.
- **Interpretation.** The analysis explains what those observations suggest about the design. It distinguishes what happened from possible explanations, considers evidence that complicates an explanation, and avoids generalizing beyond the participants and tasks studied.
- **Improvements.** Each proposed improvement addresses a finding from the study. The physical, linguistic, or conceptual classification is explained. Severity reflects the consequences for the participant, how easily they could recover, and whether the difficulty recurred.

## Common pitfalls

- **Giving away the answer.** A task names the control to use or lists the steps, so the study never tests whether participants could find their own way.
- **Explaining away difficulties.** The facilitator teaches the interface or defends the design instead of finding out why it caused trouble.
- **Reporting impressions.** Claims such as “the navigation was confusing” lack examples showing what the participant tried or expected.
- **Confusing observation with explanation.** A participant overlooks a button, and the report concludes that it needs a brighter color without considering other explanations.
- **Losing the connection to the findings.** Proposed changes are generic, reflect the author’s preferences, or simply repeat a participant’s requested solution.
- **Overstating the results.** Success with assistance is presented as independent success, or two participants’ experiences become claims about all users.

## Competency levels

- **Deficient.** Required studies are missing or inadequately documented. The reader cannot tell what participants did or how the findings were reached.
- **Emergent.** The studies yield useful observations, but weaknesses in the tasks, facilitation, or reporting limit what can be learned. Explanations and proposed improvements are only partly supported.
- **Competent.** The studies have the qualities listed above. Tasks and facilitation allow difficulties to emerge, reports explain successes and problems using specific evidence, and proposed improvements follow from the findings. Classifications are justified and limitations are acknowledged.
- **Expert.** The work meets the competent criteria and handles uncertainty especially well. The analysis weighs plausible explanations, uses observations and debriefing to distinguish between them where possible, and explains what remains unresolved. It makes a convincing case for which improvements or further tests deserve attention first.
